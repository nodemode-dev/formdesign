# The Island Press

![The workshop at the end of the harbour road](press-1.jpg)

The ferry docks twice a day, and on the days it brings paper the whole harbour road knows about it. Pallets of cream stock are wheeled up the hill on a hand cart, past the chandlery and the bakery, to a stone building with a blue door and a hand-lettered sign that simply says Press.

Inside, three people run a workshop that would not look out of place in a much larger city. There is a proofing table under the north window, a wall of type specimens pinned in neat rows, and a long bench where every job is laid out page by page before a single sheet is printed.

## A grid before anything else

Every job starts the same way, says Tomas Reyes, who founded the press eleven years ago. Before anyone thinks about pictures or colour, they agree on the grid: the margins, the number of columns, and the baseline that every line of text will sit on.

"People think the grid is a cage," he says. "It is the opposite. Once it is settled, every decision after it gets easier, because you are choosing from a few good options instead of a thousand bad ones."

> A good page lets the reader forget it was designed at all. - Tomas Reyes

## The quarterly

The press is best known for its quarterly, a forty-page magazine about life on the island: the fishing season, the school play, a recipe from whoever won the summer fair. It is set in two columns with generous margins, and each issue opens with a single photograph that runs across the full spread.

Ada Lindqvist edits it from a desk in the corner. She writes most of the headlines and all of the captions, and she is strict about length. A story that runs long is cut, not squeezed: the type size and the leading never change to make room.

"Readers notice when the texture of a page changes," she says. "They may not know why, but they feel it. If one story is set tighter than the next, the magazine stops feeling like one voice."

## Small runs, careful work

Most jobs are small. A poster for the harbour festival, a menu for the café on the quay, a booklet of walking routes for the visitor centre. The press prints them in runs of a few hundred, and each one is proofed twice: once on screen, once on paper at full size.

The third member of the team, Kofi Mensah, looks after colour. He keeps the palette for each client in a binder, with printed swatches beside the numbers, so a festival poster printed this year matches the one printed five years ago.

## What comes next

This spring the press is starting a series of evening classes for anyone on the island who wants to learn the basics: how to choose two typefaces that work together, how to place a picture so the text flows around it, how to prepare a file that a printer will not send back.

"Everyone here makes something," says Reyes. "A notice for the hall, a card for a birthday, a sign for the shop. We would like all of it to look a little more cared for."
